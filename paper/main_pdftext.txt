Abstract
Satellite-to-street view synthesis asks a model to produce a ground-level panorama from an
overhead tile. On CVUSA, we study a deliberately simple two-stage pipeline in which a predicted
semantic layout is the only scene-structure signal, and we measure which design choices matter.
Stage 1, a 1.53M-parameter PolarLayoutNet on frozen DINOv2 features, reaches 0.514 mIoU
against SegFormer pseudo-labels, +0.112 over CrossNet retrained on the same labels. Ablations
show that DINOv2 instead of VGG-16 (+0.028) and the full tile instead of a 224 px crop (+0.012)
matter, while polar versus dense mapping, a ground-plane prior and learned offsets change mIoU
by at most 0.001. In Stage 2, adding a geometrically warped aerial image to SPADE conditioning
lowers LPIPS with both predicted and oracle layouts (paired 95% bootstrap intervals exclude
zero), and swapping oracle for predicted layouts raises clean-FID from 141.0 to 182.1, the largest
gap we measure. Our clean-FID remains far behind published methods (24–41 under different
protocols); labels are pseudo-labels, building IoU is 0.21, we use one dataset and one training
seed, and the adversarial term was largely inactive after early training. Code, logs, checkpoints
and the evaluation harness are released.

1

Introduction

Generating a street-level panorama from a satellite tile is a long-standing cross-view problem [1, 3, 4].
The two views share little appearance: the aerial tile shows roofs and canopy from above, while the
panorama is dominated by sky, vegetation, buildings and the road ahead. Recent methods close
part of this gap with explicit geometry [6, 7] or diffusion models [8].
We instead study a deliberately simple and cheap pipeline whose structure is easy to measure.
Stage 1 predicts a four-class panorama layout (sky/other, vegetation, road, building) from the aerial
tile. Stage 2 renders a 256 × 512 panorama from that layout, optionally with a warped copy of the
aerial image. Because the layout is the only learned scene-structure signal, it is a bottleneck by
design. Swapping the predicted layout for an oracle layout, the segmentation of the target panorama,
measures how much that bottleneck costs.
Figure 1 shows the full pipeline on one test image. The study makes three contributions.
• An efficient layout predictor and a map of what matters. PolarLayoutNet trains
1.53M parameters on frozen DINOv2 features [12] in about four GPU-hours on a 4 GB GPU
and reaches 0.514 mIoU. Feature choice and input coverage matter; the polar geometry details
we tried do not.
• A controlled Stage 2 ladder with paired uncertainty. Five configurations separate
the generator, the layout source and warped-aerial conditioning; the oracle gap quantifies the
bottleneck.

Related Work

Cross-view GANs and geometry. Regmi and Borji [3] synthesise aerial-to-street views with
conditional GANs that also generate the target-view segmentation. SelectionGAN [4] conditions
on an image and a target semantic map and refines a coarse result with multi-channel attention
selection. S2SP [6] establishes explicit geometric correspondences and projects the satellite image to
the street viewpoint before generation, and Toker et al. [5] train street-view synthesis jointly with
cross-view retrieval. Our warped-aerial input is a much simpler, fixed polar resampling in the same
spirit.
Density- and diffusion-based methods. Sat2Density [7] learns a density field from satelliteground pairs without depth supervision, and Sat2Density++ [9] learns a neural radiance field
to render street-view panoramas and videos. CrossViewDiff [8] conditions a diffusion model on
estimated satellite scene structure and cross-view texture mapping, and its Table 1 collects the
CVUSA numbers we quote in Table 6.
Layout prediction. CrossNet [1] predicts the ground-level semantic layout from aerial imagery,
supervised by segmentations of co-located ground images, and uses aerial features to hallucinate a
plausible panorama. It is our Stage 1 baseline. CVUSA was introduced for cross-view geolocalisation
[2].
Foundation features and semantic synthesis. DINOv2 [12] provides all-purpose visual features
that work without fine-tuning; register tokens [13] remove high-norm artefacts and give smoother
feature maps. Pix2Pix [10] learns image-to-image mappings with a conditional adversarial loss.
SPADE [11] injects the semantic layout through spatially-adaptive normalisation so that the layout
is not washed out by normalisation layers. Our contribution is not a new generator but a controlled
measurement: we keep the encoder frozen, make the predicted layout an explicit bottleneck, quantify
its cost with oracle layouts, and report paired intervals for every per-image comparison.

3

Method

3.1

Data, labels and protocol

We use CVUSA [2] with its standard split of 35,532 training and 8,884 test pairs. Each pair holds a
750 × 750 aerial tile and a 1232 × 224 street-view panorama whose centre is the tile centre. The
released code calls the test split “val”; we call it the test set throughout.
CVUSA provides no street-view semantic labels. We label every panorama with SegFormer-B0
[14] fine-tuned on ADE20K and map its classes to four: sky, vegetation, road and building (the
mapping is listed in Section B). The four classes are coarse groups rather than pure categories:
class 0 combines sky and water with every ADE20K class we did not assign elsewhere (for example
person, pole and signboard), so we call it sky/other; class 1 groups vegetation with natural terrain
such as mountain, rock, sand, hill and land; class 2 includes vehicles and bare earth; and class 3
includes other built structures such as fences, railings and bridges, which may partly explain its low
IoU. Relabelling 44,416 pairs took 132.2 minutes. These pseudo-labels are the Stage 1 targets and
the Stage 2 oracle layouts, and all mIoU numbers use them. For Stage 1 model selection we hold
out a 2,000-image minival set from the training split (seed 0) and train on the remaining 33,532.

3.3

Stage 2: layout-to-panorama generators

Generators. The Pix2Pix U-Net baseline [10] (54.42M parameters) maps the one-hot layout to
RGB. It is warm-started from step 197,766 (epoch 38) of an earlier run on a previous label version
(from our course project) and fine-tuned for 15 epochs with λL1 = 100 and no perceptual loss.
The SPADE generator [11] (92.09M parameters) starts from the conditioning map downsampled
to 8 × 16, applies seven SPADE residual blocks with five 2× nearest-neighbour upsamplings, and
outputs 256 × 512 RGB; every normalisation layer is modulated by the resized conditioning map
(hidden width 128). There is no noise input, so the generator is deterministic.
Warped-aerial conditioning. We resample the aerial RGB tile into panorama geometry with a
fixed polar grid: the tile centre maps to the bottom row, the tile edge to the horizon row, azimuth
runs clockwise, and north sits at the centre column. The orientation was calibrated once on 400
training pairs by matching warped and panorama road masks. Rows above the horizon are set to
zero. The three warped channels are concatenated with the four layout channels (layout resized
with nearest neighbour, aerial bilinearly), which adds 0.09M parameters (92.18M in total). Figure 8
shows two warps.
Losses and discriminator. A conditional PatchGAN discriminator D (2.77M parameters) sees
the layout concatenated with a real or generated image; it receives the layout only, not the warped
aerial. We use least-squares GAN losses [20] with a smoothed real target of 0.9,

3.4

End-to-end inference

At test time Stage 1 logits are bilinearly upsampled to 256 × 512, converted by argmax to a one-hot
layout in [−1, 1], and passed to the generator together with the warped aerial image. Stage 2 never
sees a predicted layout in training, so Configs C and E also measure this train-test shift.

Experimental Setup

Metrics. Stage 1 reports pixel accuracy, per-class IoU and mIoU over all 8,884 test panoramas
at 224 × 1232. For Stage 2 we compute, per test image at 256 × 512, SSIM, PSNR, LPIPS
with AlexNet features [15] and a Laplacian sharpness gap |Var(∇2 Y ) − Var(∇2 Ŷ )| on 8-bit luma
Y . This gap is our own measure and is not the SD metric of prior work. Set-level clean-FID
[17, 16] and KID [18] use all 8,884 generated and real panoramas. An older torchvision-Inception
harness, which we call legacy FID, appears only in Section D. Per-image SSIM uses scikit-image
structural_similarity on uint8 RGB after mapping generator outputs from [−1, 1] to eightbit RGB, with channel_axis=2, data_range=255, default win_size=7 and uniform filter weights
(gaussian_weights=False). PSNR uses scikit-image peak_signal_noise_ratio on the same uint8
RGB tensors with data_range=255. LPIPS uses the lpips package with AlexNet (net=’alex’),
leaving inputs in [−1, 1]. clean-FID and KID call cleanfid with mode=’clean’ and batch size 32
on folders of 256 × 512 PNG RGB images written by final_eval.py; clean-FID applies its own
resizing and normalisation internally [17].
Uncertainty. Each model was trained once, so our intervals capture test-set sampling, not training
variance. Per-image metrics get 95% percentile intervals from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of test
images (seed 0); for D vs B and E vs C we bootstrap the per-image paired differences. FID and
KID have no intervals. We call a difference significant only when its 95% interval excludes zero.
Differences quoted in the text are computed from unrounded values, so they can differ by up to
0.001 (0.1 for FID) from differences of the rounded table entries.
Configurations. In Table 1, B and C share one checkpoint, as do D and E, so C−B and E−D
isolate the layout source. D−B and E−C isolate warped-aerial conditioning up to the training-run
differences noted in Section 5.3. Stage 2 had no separate validation split. Several checkpoints of the
same runs were scored on the test split during development (Section D), and the Pix2Pix, SPADE
and aerial configurations were compared on the same test images while they were being developed,
so test data may have influenced which runs and settings we kept; each configuration reports the
last checkpoint its run saved.
Compute. Summed over the logs, Stage 1 used 27.6 GPU-hours, Stage 2 16.3 and the final
evaluation 1.70, 45.6 GPU-hours in total; relabelling adds 2.20. Section C gives the per-run
breakdown, the GPUs named in the logs and what was not recorded.

5

Results

5.1

Stage 1 layout prediction

PolarLayoutNet reaches 0.514 mIoU and 0.745 pixel accuracy (Table 2). Against CrossNet retrained
on the same pseudo-labels, the gain is +0.112 mIoU, with every class improving (road +0.132,
building +0.129, vegetation +0.129, sky +0.056). Against the original CrossNet checkpoint the
gain is +0.214, but that checkpoint never saw our label definition (it scores 0.454 on its own labels),
so this larger number should not be read as an architecture effect. The retrained baseline is, if
anything, favoured: its checkpoint was selected on a 1,024-image subset of the test split.
Figure 3 shows the first three test images. The original CrossNet predicts building where the
labels have vegetation, matching its vegetation IoU of 0.026, and the retrained CrossNet produces
flat, nearly horizontal class boundaries. Our predictions follow the road shape and the vegetation
skyline more closely but still miss small buildings, which is consistent with a building IoU of 0.209.
Takeaway. A small head on frozen DINOv2 features exceeds a retrained CrossNet by +0.112 mIoU
on identical labels, at a training cost of about four GPU-hours.

5.4

The oracle gap

The largest effect in the study is the layout source. Moving from oracle to predicted layouts with
the same checkpoint raises clean-FID by 41.1 (D to E: 141.0 to 182.1), LPIPS by 0.085 and KID by
0.047, and lowers PSNR by 2.46 dB and SSIM by 0.050. Without the aerial input the gap is similar
(39.6 clean-FID, B to C). In clean-FID the oracle gap is 2.9 times the warped-aerial gain, and in
LPIPS it is 3.2 times. Part of the gap may come from the train-test shift in Section 3.4, because
the generator never saw predicted layouts during training; the two causes are not separated here.
Takeaway. The largest effect we measure is the layout source: a perfect layout would be worth
roughly three times what the aerial image adds, although we cannot separate Stage 1 error from the
train–test mismatch.

5.5

Qualitative results and failure cases

Figure 5 samples Config E by its own LPIPS quantiles. On open roads (best and 25th percentile)
the predicted layout recovers the road shape and the generator produces a plausible, blurry scene.
At the median and 75th percentile, the predicted vegetation skyline differs from the oracle and the
outputs inherit that error; D shares E’s weights but receives the oracle layout. The worst case is
a forest view in which both layouts are almost entirely vegetation and every generator returns a
uniform blur; Figure 7 shows six uncurated samples; the next three worst cases (test images 2955,
668 and 35) are also dense vegetation scenes with a nearly uniform predicted layout (LPIPS 1.012,
1.010 and 1.001). All samples are far blurrier than the real panoramas.
Takeaway. Visible errors track layout errors, and the low-texture outputs explain why pixel metrics
look acceptable while clean-FID stays high.

6

Training Diagnostics

Our first Pix2Pix fine-tuning warm-started both generator and discriminator from the earlier run,
and its discriminator was saturated from the first step (Figure 6). An instrumented FP32 run
(warm-started weights, a fresh optimiser state and one generator step per discriminator step) shows
the mechanism: at step one the discriminator output was 0.899 on real images, almost exactly the
smoothed target of 0.9, and 0.000 on generated images. Over 20 diagnostic lines up to step 500 the
real mean stayed between 0.876 and 0.910, the fake output never exceeded 0.009 in magnitude, and
every logged discriminator loss was below 0.05, the lower edge of the script’s healthy band. The
mixed-precision run behaved the same way, which rules out mixed-precision underflow as the cause.
A third diagnostic run in FP32 changed two things at once, a randomly initialised discriminator

left: the adversarial term was largely inactive after the first epochs.
Takeaway. A warm-started discriminator can saturate before training starts. Random initialisation
(together with three generator steps per discriminator step) restores the early dynamics but not the
late collapse, so the reported generators should be read as mostly reconstruction-trained.

7

Discussion and Limitations

Where the pipeline stands. Our end-to-end clean-FID of 182.1 is far behind the published FID
range of 24–41 (Table 6), and we expect protocol differences to explain only part of a gap this large.
Our higher SSIM and PSNR do not indicate better synthesis: these metrics reward smooth outputs,
and our samples are visibly blurrier (Figure 5).
10

Limitations. All labels are SegFormer pseudo-labels, so mIoU measures agreement with another
model rather than with human annotation, and building IoU is only 0.21; there is no human
evaluation of the synthesised panoramas. We use one dataset, CVUSA, which covers the United
States, and make no claim about other datasets or regions. The 1232 × 224 panoramas are resized to
256 × 512 (height × width), which changes their aspect ratio. Each configuration was trained once,
so our intervals cover test-image sampling only. The A-to-B and warped-aerial comparisons carry
the confounds stated in Section 5.3, the adversarial term was largely inactive after the first epochs,
and Stage 2 was trained only on oracle layouts. We also did not train a generator conditioned on the
warped aerial tile alone, so we cannot say whether the layout stage helps relative to direct synthesis.
Future work. The oracle gap points first to a better Stage 1 and to training Stage 2 on predicted
layouts, which would also separate layout error from train–test mismatch. A generator conditioned
only on the warped aerial tile would test whether the layout stage helps at all. A diffusion refiner
with ControlNet-style layout conditioning [22] could add the missing texture, and a second dataset
and several training seeds would test how far the findings generalise.

8

Conclusion

A 1.53M-parameter layout head on frozen DINOv2 features reaches 0.514 mIoU on CVUSA pseudolabels, +0.112 over a retrained CrossNet; the backbone and input coverage matter, the polar
mapping details do not. A warped aerial image lowers LPIPS with both layout sources, but the
step from oracle to predicted layouts costs 41.1 clean-FID, the largest gap we measure, which points
to Stage 1 quality, train–test mismatch, or both as the first things to improve in this design. The
released code, logs and checkpoints give a cheap, fully measured baseline against which better layout
prediction can be tested.
